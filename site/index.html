Predicting hERG blockers

A cardiotoxicity screen built from ChEMBL bioactivity data — and a demonstration of how much of a machine-learning score in drug discovery can be an artefact of how the data was split.

The finding

Random split— → Scaffold split— Difference—

The same random forest, the same compounds, the same features. The only change is that a scaffold split keeps chemical series whole, so a test compound never has a close analogue sitting in the training set.

That difference is the portion of the apparent performance that was recognising molecules it had effectively already seen. A random split is the default in most tutorials, and on this dataset it overstates the model by that margin. The number a medicinal chemist should care about is the lower one.

Reading past the headline number

—

For a safety screen the asymmetry matters more than the AUROC. Missing a blocker (a false negative) advances a compound that can prolong the QT interval; a false positive only costs you a molecule. A model with high sensitivity and mediocre specificity may be the right one to deploy even when another ties it on AUROC.

Data. ChEMBL target CHEMBL240 (hERG). Activity threshold 10 µM. Features are Morgan/ECFP4 fingerprints, radius 2, 2048 bits.

Method. Bemis–Murcko scaffolds, stratified so class balance is preserved across the split. Every number here is on the held-out test set.
